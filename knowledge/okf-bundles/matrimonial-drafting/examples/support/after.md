# Pendente lite support certification (developed)

Anonymized reference sample. Dates and financial figures are fictional. Names and identifying details are generalized. Full narrative retained; referenced exhibits are excluded.

**Plaintiff v Defendant**

Client is Defendant. This is a working document, not a confirmed filed version.

I, Defendant, do hereby certify as follows:

1\. I am the Defendant in the above-captioned matter and as such I am fully familiar with the fact and circumstances herein. I submit this Certification in support of my Notice of Motion seeking pendente lite financial support and other relief. I have attempted to resolve these issues with Plaintiff, through counsel, without court intervention, but at this point Plaintiff seems intent on minimizing the marital lifestyle and controlling the funds available to me while reducing his financial contributions in ways that do not reflect how we lived during our marriage. I cannot meet the expenses that were routinely paid during our marriage based on the funds Plaintiff has chosen to make available, while Plaintiff controls the resources needed to pay the expenses. I am requesting that the Court establish fair, interim support and payment obligations so that my financial circumstances are not determined solely by Plaintiff while this case is pending. I seek direct payment of the Schedule A and B expenses in Plaintiff’s Case Information Statement, \$20,000 per month for my Schedule C needs, a \$100,000 litigation fund from marital assets, exclusive occupancy of our Municipality A home, and meaningful disclosures. I should not have to negotiate access to marital funds one bill at a time with the spouse who controls them.

2\. By way of brief background, Plaintiff and I were married on February 7, 2033. I am fifty-seven (57) years old and Plaintiff is fifty-six (56) years old. No children were born of our marriage. I am a licensed professional, but my practice is limited and by virtue of our marital finances, Plaintiff’s earnings and significant accumulated marital wealth, I have not needed to maintain a full-time practice or generate significant income in order support our marital lifestyle. My 2053 practice income was approximately \$42,000, according to my preliminary accounting, and I will provide the controlling tax and CIS figures in the finalized disclosures.

3\. Plaintiff is a financial professional who served as the senior executive of Former Employer, a subsidiary of Former Parent Company, earning a gross annual income in excess of \$2,000,000.00. In March 2048, Plaintiff resigned from that position and for a period of time remained unemployed during a period of broader economic disruption. Plaintiff is now employed with Current Employer, earning more than \$600,000.00 gross per year plus an annual bonus. Plaintiff’s Case Information Statement (“CIS”) is attached hereto as Exhibit “A.”

4\. Plaintiff has been the primary wage-earner throughout our marriage. Plaintiff also took primary responsibility for managing our financial affairs. Plaintiff controlled our bank accounts, decided how income was deposited and spent, paid the household bills, managed our investments and other assets, and handled our debts and tax matters. Plaintiff determined when expenses would be paid, which accounts would be used, and how much money would be available for our household and discretionary spending. I relied on Plaintiff to manage those matters and to fund the lifestyle we maintained during the marriage. I did not independently monitor every account, investment, transfer or bill because Plaintiff handled those matters and the system worked for our household. As a result, Plaintiff has a far more complete understanding of our income, assets, liabilities, and historical spending than I do. However, now that this matter is in litigation, Plaintiff’s control over those finances also gives him the ability to unilaterally decide, unless the Court intervenes, which of the expenses he historically paid will continue to be paid and how much money I will have available to meet my needs. I am being asked to explain and justify my spending while still trying to identify accounts, obtain supporting records and understand the full picture of our finances. The imbalance is unmistakable: Plaintiff knows where the money is and can move it, while I must request information from him to complete a sworn CIS and seek his cooperation to meet ordinary expenses.

5\. Although Plaintiff managed our finances, he did not restrict my access to or use of marital funds. Throughout our marriage, I was free to use our credit cards, debit cards, charge accounts, and available cash to pay expenses, make purchases, and maintain the lifestyle we had established. Plaintiff did not give me a spending allowance, impose a monthly budget, require me to obtain his approval before making purchases, or question me about expenditures. I did not have to ask whether funds would be available before making a purchase, paying a bill or using a credit card. That was our established financial arrangement during the marriage. As I will further set forth herein, the limits Plaintiff now seeks to impose are a substantial change from how I was permitted to manage my day-to-day needs and expenses while we lived together.

**Marital Lifestyle and Resources**

6\. As stated above, Plaintiff and I have accumulated substantial wealth during our marriage. We own a home in Municipality A, New Jersey (the “Former Marital Home”) that we purchased in 2043 with an estimated value of \$5.0 million. In addition, for years we maintained a second home in State B. In 2052, we sold the State B property and purchased \[Second Residence\] in Municipality B, New Jersey, which Plaintiff now identifies as his residence on his CIS. We lived with the costs, conveniences and choices associated with two (2) substantial properties.

7\. Plaintiff and I enjoyed a substantial marital lifestyle. We maintained, furnished and improved both homes, frequently dined at restaurants, and traveled several times a year. Our trips included destinations throughout the United States including in State D, State E, State F, and State C, as well as international destinations in Country A, Country B, and other countries. Designer clothing, handbags, shoes, fine jewelry, vehicles, and recreational equipment were regular features of our lives. Our household spending was far from confined to a modest budget for basic necessities.

8\. Plaintiff participated fully in that lifestyle and devoted substantial resources to his own interests, including vehicles, recreation, and related equipment. Plaintiff is an automobile enthusiast and a member of Recreation Club A, an exclusive automotive club with a substantial initiation fee and continuing dues and expenses. Plaintiff’s spending on these interests is part of the marital lifestyle the Court should consider when evaluating the limited budget Plaintiff is now attempting to advance for litigation purposes.

9\. Jewelry was also a longstanding part of our marriage. We patronized Jewelry Retailer A over many years and it was not uncommon to spend \$40,000 + per year on jewelry. As I will further explain herein, Plaintiff now challenges an anniversary jewelry purchase as part of what seems to be Plaintiff’s litigation strategy, but that purchase should be viewed in the context of our established spending on jewelry, rather than treated as an isolated expense outside our lifestyle.

10\. Plaintiff’s September 2054 CIS reports gross assets of approximately \$12,137,572, including stated values of \$5.0 million for the Former Marital Home and \$1,400,000 for the Municipality B residence, as well as substantial brokerage, retirement, and insurance assets. I recognize that these are Plaintiff’s reported values, not appraisals, and that gross assets are not the same as cash available for monthly expenses. They nevertheless provide important context for the scale of our marital finances and the lifestyle that Plaintiff’s proposed monthly budget fails to reflect.

11\. Our tax returns show the financial circumstances in which we lived before Plaintiff’s employment changed. Our 2051 joint return reports approximately \$1,160,000 in adjusted gross income, and our 2052 joint return reports approximately \$1,184,000 in total income. (See Exhibit’s B and C respectively) Those figures come from different lines of the returns and are not a year-to-year comparison of the same measure. They do, however, show that substantial income supported our household in both years. In 2052, Plaintiff’s Schedule C also reported approximately \$318,750 in gross consulting receipts and \$313,190 in net consulting income.

12\. The draft 2053 return reports approximately \$190,000 in total income during a materially different employment year. Plaintiff continued to report business activity in 2053, while his new corporate position had not yet produced a full year of wages. Plaintiff’s present disclosures identify a \$600,000 annual base salary and a \$200,000 target bonus. Plaintiff’s August 14, 2054 pay statement reports approximately \$438,482 in year-to-date gross compensation, including bonus and back-pay entries. I recognize that these figures require reconciliation: the bonus is a target and amounts already reflected in his pay statement should not be added to his salary a second time. Even with those qualifications, Plaintiff’s current employment reflects a substantially greater earning capacity than mine.

13\. Saving and investing were also part of our financial lives. The available Bank B money-market records show balances of approximately \$325,460 at the end of 2051, \$375,500 at the end of 2052, and \$149,518 in January 2054. Those balances changed as funds moved among accounts and were used for expenses and property transactions and likely additional transactions by Plaintiff that I am unaware of at this moment. I do not suggest that every transfer represents new income or additional savings. Plaintiff’s September 2054 CIS nevertheless reports approximately \$12.14 million in gross assets, including substantial retirement and brokerage holdings, but fails to list any savings/investment expense on his CIS. I understand that gross asset values do not establish the amount of cash available to pay support.

14\. I am not asking the Court to assume that our 2052 income will recur in 2054 or to set support by simply adding together every figure above. I ask the Court to consider the lifestyle supported by our historical income, our pattern of saving and investing, Plaintiff’s current compensation, and our respective resources when determining reasonable pendente lite support. That history also provides the necessary context for evaluating Plaintiff’s present CIS. Plaintiff’s current budget reflects the expenses he has chosen to report; it does not, by itself, describe the standard of living established over our twenty-one-year marriage.

15\. Plaintiff’s CIS reports \$8,314 per month in Schedule A housing expenses, \$1,958 per month in Schedule B transportation expenses, \$16,650 per month in Schedule C personal and family expenses, and \$5,704 per month as the monthly average of nonrecurring expenses, for a total of \$32,626 per month. (See Exhibit “A”) Even if those figures accurately describe Plaintiff’s present spending, they do not account for the full range of expenditures that characterized our marriage. In particular, I see no meaningful provision for saving or investing, although accumulating assets was a significant part of our financial life during the higher-income years.

16\. Several of Plaintiff’s individual budget entries illustrate the same problem. Plaintiff lists \$850 per month for clothing, \$272 per month for gifts, including jewelry, \$298 per month for vacations, and \$306 per month for clubs, sports, and hobbies. Together, those categories total \$1,726 per month, or \$20,712 per year, which does not reasonably reflect our historical spending on designer clothing, handbags and shoes, jewelry, domestic and international travel, or Plaintiff’s vehicles, recreation, equipment, and club membership. Jewelry purchases alone far exceed Plaintiff’s figures. I recognize that past discretionary spending must be considered alongside our present means. But Plaintiff’s limited current entries should not be accepted as a complete account of the marital lifestyle when the Court determines appropriate interim support.

17\. Attached hereto as Exhibit “D” is my Case Information Statement. Again, my knowledge of the specific details of our marital finances is limited at this time. I do know that Plaintiff has significantly underestimated a number of our expenses and failed to even list other expenses. My Case Information Statement represents my best estimate of expenses based upon a review of the limited information that I have available to me at this time.

**Circumstances Upon Separation**

18\. On or about August 3, 2054, Plaintiff told me that he wanted to end our marriage. Plaintiff left Former Marital Home on or about August 6, 2054. Later that month, Plaintiff asked to return to the Former Marital Home to collect clothing and other items he needed for work. We agreed that I would be away while he did so.

19\. When I returned, I observed that Plaintiff had removed clothing and personal items and left several boxes of my belongings. I also noticed that the filing cabinet we had kept in the basement was gone. During the marriage, we both used that unlocked cabinet to store household financial records and had access to it. Plaintiff had not told me that he intended to move the cabinet or its contents.

20\. On September 9, 2054, Plaintiff’s attorney sent my counsel Plaintiff’s Case Information Statement and a letter criticizing purchases I had made. The letter stated that Plaintiff intended to unilaterally redirect his salary deposits away from our joint checking account and pay only those expenses Plaintiff considered “marital bills.” Plaintiff’s attorney also sent a proposed “cut-off date” agreement, which I did not sign. The clear message was that Plaintiff would unilaterally alter our long-standing status quo and would be making his own determination about which expenses he would pay, without an agreement or an assessment of the funds I would need to meet my expenses. (See Exhibit “E”)

21\. Plaintiff confirmed the change in a September 13, 2054, letter to me. Plaintiff identified an approximately \$16,000 Jewelry Retailer B purchase and more than \$24,000 in other recent purchases, told me to pay for that spending from my business account, and stated that he had redirected his salary away from our joint account. Plaintiff also warned that there might not be enough money to cover the next automatic payment on the Bank D credit card. (See Exhibit “F”) Because that credit card and our household bills had historically been paid through accounts funded and managed by Plaintiff, his warning left me uncertain whether historic, established payments would clear.

22\. My attorney responded on September 14, 2054. My counsel explained that the marital financial arrangement encompassed more than fixed household bills and that I needed dependable access to funds while support remained unresolved. My counsel also conveyed my willingness to discuss reasonable parameters for spending. Plaintiff nevertheless proceeded with his decision to redirect his income. (See Exhibit “G”)

23\. Plaintiff filed his Complaint for Divorce on September 18, 2054. Shortly thereafter, with Plaintiff’s permission, I stayed at \[Second Residence\] while he was away on September 19th and 20th. While there, I saw the filing cabinet in a bedroom Plaintiff used as a study or studio. The cabinet was unlocked. I was familiar with the room and had used it during the marriage. I removed financial records from the cabinet, including original bank and credit-card statements accumulated during the marriage. Unfortunately, most of the records were historical and did not provide sufficient information as to our present and current finances.

24\. The limited records available to me show a corporate payroll deposit of approximately \$16,302.46 into our joint account near the end of August 2054. Plaintiff subsequently redirected his payroll deposits. I need the complete statements for that account and the account receiving his subsequent pay to establish the dates and amounts involved. In the meantime, I have been left uncertain about the funds available to cover expenses that were previously paid through our established household accounts.

25\. Plaintiff’s counsel’s September 28, 2054 letter acknowledges that Plaintiff transferred \$1,000 and then \$73,762 from the Bank B money-market account into an account in his own name for a total of \$74,762. Counsel described the transfers as a prudent reserve for taxes and expenses. I do not claim that the money has disappeared or that reserving funds for documented obligations would necessarily be improper. I was not consulted about the amount or the transfer, however, and I have not received complete records for the account into which the funds were deposited. I ask that the funds remain identifiable and that their use be documented. (See Exhibit “H”)

26\. The same September 28th letter from Plaintiff’s counsel attributes approximately \$67,342 in recent spending and withdrawals to me, compares that figure to the limited lifestyle categories in Plaintiff’s Case Information Statement, and suggests that Plaintiff will transfer money when he considers it necessary for my “normal” expenses. I am willing to account for my spending and to observe reasonable limits that apply to both of us. I cannot, however, manage my expenses through an arrangement in which Plaintiff alone decides what is “normal,” when to deposit funds, and how much will be available when a bill comes due. Plaintiff’s own substantial spending interests have long been part of our marital lifestyle, as I described above.

27\. Plaintiff historically earned substantially more than I did, managed our accounts, and ensured that household bills and credit cards were paid. My income cannot simply replace the deposits he redirected. Since Plaintiff changed those arrangements, I have had to seek information about how expenses will be paid and worry whether established payments will clear. I also need funds to review the accounts and participate meaningfully in this litigation. I cannot speak to Plaintiff’s intentions, but the financial uncertainty and strain caused by these unilateral decisions are real.

28\. I acknowledge that the purchases Plaintiff identified were significant, albeit clearly consistent with past expenses and purchases, and I am prepared to address them through my Case Information Statement and the account records. I would also comply with reasonable, reciprocal limits on unusually large discretionary purchases while this case is pending. Those purchases, however, do not eliminate my need for regular support or justify leaving the household payment system uncertain and solely at Plaintiff’s discretion. I ask the Court to establish dependable pendente lite support and clear financial arrangements so that I can meet my reasonable expenses while this matter proceeds and assure that I too have necessary funds to meaningfully participate in this litigation.

**Pendente Lite Support**

29\. Plaintiff’s decision to redirect his income and pay only the expenses he chooses has left our established marital status quo and payment arrangements uncertain. I ask the Court to replace that unilateral arrangement with a clear temporary order identifying what Plaintiff must pay, what funds will be available to me, and how both parties will account for expenditures while this case proceeds.

30\. First, I request that Plaintiff continue paying the Schedule A, housing, and Schedule B, transportation, expenses identified in his Case Information Statement as these expenses become due. Plaintiff reports those expenses at approximately \$8,314 and \$1,958 per month, respectively, or \$10,272 combined. (See Exhibit “A”) I ask that the order identify the actual bills to be paid, eliminate duplicate or discontinued charges, require necessary insurance and utilities to remain in effect, and require Plaintiff to provide proof of payment. To the extent these figures include expenses for the Municipality B property Plaintiff occupies, those payments should be identified as his expenses and not treated as cash support provided to me.

31\. Second, I request \$20,000 per month in cash pendente lite support, in addition to Plaintiff’s direct payment of the identified Schedule A and B obligations. Plaintiff’s own Case Information Statement reports \$16,650 per month in recurring Schedule C expenses and an additional \$5,704 per month as the average of nonrecurring expenses for a combined \$22,354 before any separately identified savings allowance. My CIS reports \$26,590 per month in expenses with a number of expenses left to be determined. My \$20,000 per month request is a measured interim amount in light of those figures, the historical lifestyle described above, and Plaintiff’s current compensation. It does not attempt to reproduce every unusually expensive period of our marriage.

32\. I also ask the Court to address savings expressly when evaluating temporary support and the parties’ access to funds. Saving and investing were part of our financial life, yet Plaintiff’s proposed budget makes no meaningful provision for me to preserve liquidity while he continues to control the destination of his earnings and access to investment accounts. I request disclosure of ongoing retirement and investment contributions and consideration of a reasonable savings component or other access to liquid funds consistent with our present means. I do not ask the Court to assume that we saved the same amount every month or to order matching contributions without reviewing the records.

33\. Relatedly, I request an accounting of the \$74,762 transferred to an account in Plaintiff’s name, including the date, source, destination, and subsequent use of those funds. I am not asking the Court to presume that the transfer was improper. I ask that both parties have accurate information about available funds and be subject to the same reasonable financial disclosure and preservation requirements.

**Litigation Fund**

34\. I need access to funds to participate meaningfully in this divorce. Plaintiff has substantially greater current earning power and greater access to the banking, investment, and compensation information that must be reviewed. My counsel must prepare and evaluate financial disclosures, obtain and analyze account and credit-card records, address support and equitable distribution, and, if necessary, retain financial professionals. I cannot undertake that work on an equal footing if I must seek Plaintiff’s approval before paying my own counsel or experts.

35\. I request an immediate \$100,000 advance for counsel fees and reasonable expert expenses from an identified liquid marital account, with the source, use, and ultimate allocation of those funds subject to accounting and determination at final judgment. This is a request for litigation funding, not a request to distribute the entire marital estate now. Counsel will submit an affidavit of services and a projected budget supporting the amount requested. Without an advance, I face the choice of using funds needed for living expenses or foregoing the work necessary to understand and protect my interests.

**Occupancy and Preservation of Property**

36\. I have continued living at \[Former Marital Residence\] in Municipality A (“Former Marital Home”) since Plaintiff moved out. Plaintiff has identified \[Second Residence\] in Municipality B as his address and appears to be residing there on a full-time basis. I request exclusive occupancy of the Former Marital Home, while this action is pending, without prejudice to either party’s ultimate ownership claim. Maintaining separate residences will provide continuity and reduce the opportunity for conflict. I ask that neither Plaintiff nor I enter the residence occupied by the other without prior agreement, except in a genuine emergency or as otherwise ordered, and that reasonable arrangements be made on written notice to retrieve specifically identified personal property.

37\. Finally, I ask that both Plaintiff and I be directed to preserve marital property and the contents of the residences. Neither Plaintiff nor I should conceal, transfer, substantially encumber, or dispose of marital assets, or make extraordinary withdrawals, except for ordinary living expenses, reasonable litigation costs, written agreement, or further court order. There should also be mutual and full disclosure of all accounts receiving income or transfers and the production of periodic account statements. These requirements should apply equally to Plaintiff and me. I seek transparency and preservation while the case is pending, so that the any unilateral control of funds determines the practical outcome before any claims of either Plaintiff or I can be heard.

**Information Needed to Verify Parties’ Finances**

38\. I have tried to verify the expenses Plaintiff attributes to \[Second Residence\] in Municipality B before relying on these expenses in my sworn financial disclosures and Case Information Statement. I requested the records supporting Plaintiff’s monthly calculation on October 5, 2054, and renewed the request on October 7th. On October 8th, Plaintiff sent an e-mail with a spreadsheet, but did not provide all of the underlying records that I had requested. I sought those documents to check the figures, not because I was unwilling to consider the information Plaintiff provided. (See Exhibit “I”)

39\. Specifically, I requested the property tax, insurance, utility, internet, maintenance, and renovation records for the Municipality B property, along with identification of the accounts from which those expenses were paid. The Municipality B property was acquired in 2052. The source documents would allow me to distinguish ongoing monthly costs from one-time expenditures and to verify the amounts reported in Plaintiff’s Case Information Statement.

40\. On October 13th, Plaintiff responded that he had reclassified certain expenses in his spreadsheet but that the total remained unchanged. Plaintiff stated that most expenses had been paid from the joint account, while his credit card and the Municipality B expenses were being paid from his personal account as of September. Plaintiff also identified different account holders and payment sources for certain Municipality A services. Plaintiff’s response shows why the underlying records matter: the categories and payment arrangements have changed, and a spreadsheet alone does not establish which expenses are current, who is paying them, or whether an item has been counted more than once. (See Exhibit “J”)

41\. Plaintiff has supplied some tax materials, sample bills, and spreadsheets, and I acknowledge that production. Material gaps nevertheless remain. I need the bills and account records supporting the expense figures to prepare an accurate Case Information Statement and to evaluate the temporary financial obligations. I should not have to certify an expense merely because Plaintiff has entered it on a spreadsheet when the supporting record is available to Plaintiff, but has not been provided.

42\. The records produced thus far have also raised questions about Plaintiff’s business income, accounts that may require further identification, and the reporting of certain assets and liabilities. I do not ask the Court to draw a conclusion of concealment from my preliminary review. I ask that Plaintiff provide complete, current records for the relevant bank and brokerage accounts, compensation and business income, and tax filings; identify the accounts receiving his postseparation earnings and transferred funds; and update his Case Information Statement as necessary. That information will permit both parties and the Court to evaluate support on a reliable record rather than on competing, unverified summaries.

**Conclusion**

43\. Since our separation, Plaintiff has redirected the paycheck that funded our household, transferred savings into an account under his control, and proposed that he alone decide which of my expenses warrant payment. At the same time, Plaintiff’s Case Information Statement gives little weight to the travel, clothing, jewelry, recreation, and saving that formed part of our marital lifestyle. Whatever Plaintiff’s intentions, the result is that he controls the timing and availability of funds while I face uncertainty about meeting ordinary expenses and paying for this litigation.

44\. I do not ask the Court to penalize Plaintiff for seeking a divorce or to authorize unrestricted spending by either of us. I ask for a dependable interim arrangement based on our actual financial history and present resources. Regular support, access to the records underlying Plaintiff’s figures, and safeguards that apply to both parties would allow us to meet reasonable needs and preserve property while the case is resolved.

45\. I therefore respectfully request that the Court direct Plaintiff to pay the verified Schedule A and B obligations as they become due; pay me \$20,000 per month in pendente lite cash support toward my Schedule C and nonrecurring expenses, with reasonable savings capacity considered separately; make \$100,000 available for counsel fees and appropriate expert expenses from a liquid marital asset, subject to accounting and final allocation; continue my exclusive occupancy of the Municipality A residence during this action; impose reciprocal protections against extraordinary transfers or dissipation of marital property; and require prompt production of the underlying financial records needed to verify expenses, account for transfers, and evaluate both parties’ Case Information Statements. These measures would replace uncertainty and unilateral financial decisions with a clear arrangement for both parties pending final resolution.

**CERTIFICATION**

I hereby certify that the forgoing statements made by me are true. I understand that if any of the forgoing statements made by me and willfully false, I am subject to punishment.

**Defendant**

Dated: October 30, 2054
