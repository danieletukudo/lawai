Alimony is governed by N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23(b), which requires the Court to consider a series of enumerated factors in determining both the type and amount of alimony that is fair and equitable under the circumstances. Plaintiff respectfully addresses each statutory factor below, not as an abstract checklist, but as applied to the lived realities of this marriage and its dissolution.

This factor-by-factor analysis demonstrates that limited duration alimony, for a defined term of four years and with income imputed to Defendant, is not only reasonable, but compelled by the statute.

> **1. The Actual Need and Ability of the Parties to Pay**

Plaintiff has the ability to pay some level of alimony, but that ability is not limitless and must be viewed in context. Plaintiff is the sole wage earner and has been for the entirety of the marriage and separation. Defendant’s claimed need, however, is overstated and self-inflicted. His financial dependence is the result of voluntary unemployment and a prolonged refusal to engage in meaningful work or retraining despite being fully capable of doing so. Alimony is intended to address unavoidable economic disparity, not to perpetuate dependency where a spouse has the ability to contribute to his own support.

Plaintiff’s proposal balances her ability to pay with Defendant’s obligation to become self-supporting, consistent with the statute’s purpose.

> **2. The Duration of the Marriage**

While the parties were married in name until physical separation in October 2023, the functional marriage ended years earlier. By mid-2022, the parties had agreed that the marriage was over. What followed was a period of forced proximity caused by Covid-related travel restrictions and Plaintiff’s binding international employment contract.

**COUNSEL FEES**

**Legal Standard**

An award of counsel fees in a matrimonial action is governed by Rule 5:3-5(c) and N.J.S.A. 2A:34-23, which direct the Court to consider, among other factors, the financial circumstances of the parties, the reasonableness and good faith of the positions advanced, the extent of fees incurred by both parties, the results obtained, and whether either party’s conduct unnecessarily increased the cost of litigation.

The purpose of a counsel fee award is not punitive. Rather, it is to level the playing field and to ensure that one party is not unfairly burdened by litigation costs as a result of the other party’s conduct or superior access to resources. See Mani v. Mani, 183 N.J. 70 (2005); Williams v. Williams, 59 N.J. 229 (1971).

**Plaintiff’s Financial Circumstances and Litigation Burden**

Plaintiff has already borne a disproportionate financial burden throughout the marriage and the separation. As the sole wage earner, she has supported the family, funded two households, paid all child-related expenses, and directly supported Defendant during the parties’ separation. She has done so while simultaneously paying her own legal fees in an effort to move this matter toward resolution.

Although Plaintiff has the greater income, that fact alone should not obscure the reality that Defendant’s financial dependence has been prolonged and exacerbated by his litigation conduct. Plaintiff’s resources have been materially depleted not only by ongoing support obligations, but by the costs associated with Defendant’s refusal to engage in timely and meaningful resolution.

**Defendant’s Conduct and the Unnecessary Protraction of Litigation**

Defendant’s conduct throughout this matter has significantly increased Plaintiff’s counsel fees. Despite Plaintiff’s repeated good-faith efforts to resolve financial issues, including at least four offers to advance funds against equitable distribution or to divide marital accounts, Defendant refused to engage unless he received money without accounting or credit.

Even the \$50,000 advance Defendant received occurred only by way of court order, not cooperation.

This pattern reflects a lack of good faith and a strategic reliance on delay, knowing that Plaintiff would continue to shoulder the financial burden. New Jersey courts have consistently held that such conduct weighs heavily in favor of a counsel fee award. See Yueh v. Yueh, 329 N.J. Super. 447 (App. Div. 2000).
