1.  <u>**THE COURT MAY PROPERLY DENY DEFENDANT’S DEMAND FOR ALIMONY BECAUSE HE IS ABLE TO MAINTAIN HIS MARITAL LIFESTYLE WITH HIS INCOME**.</u>

Plaintiff asserts that Defendant is not a supported spouse based on the proofs of spending and his marital lifestyle. In New Jersey, "\[a\]limony is a claim arising upon divorce, which is rooted in the parties' prior \[financial\] interdependence" created during their marital relationship. <u>Reese v. Weis</u>, 430 <u>N.J. Super.</u> 552, 569 (App. Div. 2013). The goal of alimony is to assist the supported spouse to maintain a lifestyle that is comparable to that enjoyed during the marriage. <u>Lepis v. Lepis</u>*,* 83 <u>N.J</u>. 139 (1980). It is undisputed that in New Jersey “the goal of a proper alimony award is to assist the supported spouse in achieving a lifestyle that is reasonably comparable to the one enjoyed while living with the supporting spouse during the marriage.” <u>Steneken v. Steneken</u>*,* 183 <u>N.J.</u> 290, 299 (2005) (<u>quoting</u> <u>Crews v. Crews</u>, 164 <u>N.J.</u> 11, 16 (2000)). The basic principles of alimony apply to both the supported and the supporting spouse with equal force, since an appropriate alimony award must also consider “the supported spouse's ability to contribute to his or her own support . . .” <u>Crews</u>, <u>supra</u>, 164 <u>N.J.</u> at 27; <u>see also</u> <u>Aronson v. Aronson</u>*,* 245 <u>N.J. Super*.*</u> 354, 364 (App. Div. 1991). In this case, the evidence will show that Defendant not only has the ability to contribute to his own support, but he has the ability to pay for the entirety of his needs consistent with the lifestyle he enjoyed during the marriage with his own income. He is only further benefitted by the fact that he moved to Texas, where the cost of living and taxes are lower than in New Jersey so his income of \$150,000 goes further.

<u>N.J.S.A.</u> 2A:34-23(b) is the statute which addresses alimony and it sets forth fourteen (14) factors for the Court to review. The analysis of the factors set forth in <u>N.J.S.A.</u> 2A:34-23(b) are as follows:

1.  **The actual need and ability of the parties to pay**. Plaintiff will prove, by way of her expert’s testimony and financial records, that Defendant has the ability at his current income of \$150,000 to meet his marital lifestyle and pay for his status quo expenses. As the charts set forth above from [Plaintiff’s Accounting Firm] demonstrates, Defendant spent \$7,984 per month of 18.8% of the income to meet his marital lifestyle. Defendant’s income is \$12,500 per month and thus even tax affected it matches, and may even surpass, his marital lifestyle in light of the favorable cost of living in Texas. Defendant has attempted to alter his marital lifestyle to inflate his position and his demand for alimony from Plaintiff. The Court will hear testimony and financial records that prove that Defendant drastically inflated/increased his expenses and lifestyle since he moved to Texas with his girlfriend.

2.  **The duration of the marriage or civil union**. The parties were married for approximately eleven-and-a-half years.

It has long been determined that establishing the lifestyle enjoyed by the parties during their marriage is the “touchstone” in making any alimony determination. <u>Crew v. The <u>S.W.</u> Court further instructs that a determination made by the Court as to marital lifestyle must be articulated numerically. The Appellate Division, in <u>S.W. v. G.M,</u> explicitly directs that “a finding of marital lifestyle must be made by explaining the characteristics of the lifestyle and quantifying it.” <u>S.W.</u>, 462 N.J. Super. at 532. This quantification requires the Court to consider the relevant testimony and to review CISs, as well as other financial documents and relevant evidence. <u>Id.</u> The factfinder may “accept or reject any portion of the marital lifestyle presented by a party or an expert or calculate the lifestyle using any combination of the presentations.” <u>Id.</u> In this case, Plaintiff will present her testimony, financial records and proofs and the testimony of [Plaintiff’s Expert] of [Plaintiff’s Accounting Firm] to demonstrate (1) the parties’ respective incomes during the marriage and (2) that she spent a monthly average of \$34,432 compared to Defendant’s monthly average spending of \$7,984 each month.

The Court in <u>S.W.</u> makes clear that numerically articulating the marital standard does not mean, however, that the Court should simply apply the parties’ net incomes. There is <u>no indication that</u>

> the Legislature intended income equalization or a formulaic application in alimony cases, even where the parties spent the entirety of their income. Had the Legislature intended alimony be calculated through use of a formula, there would be no need for the statutory requirement that the trial court address all the statutory factors. The Legislature declined to adopt a formulaic approach to the calculation of alimony.
>
> \[<u>Id.</u> at 533 (citation omitted).\]

This instruction by the Court makes clear that income is not equalized and there is no easy formula or entitlement by a spouse. The <u>S.W.</u> Court makes clear that, during a marriage, some expenses incurred are not incurred by both parties, or by either party in some circumstances, such as child related costs. The Appellate Division in 2021 issued a landmark opinion in a post-judgment matter on issue of cohabitation in its decision of <u>Temple v. Temple</u>, 468 <u>N.J. Super.</u> 364 (App. Div. 2021). The Court, after reviewing the statutory factors pertinent to a determination of cohabitation, defined cohabitation as “mutually supportive, intimate, personal relationship” in which the couple has “undertaken duties and privileges that are commonly associated with marriage or civil union.” This is consistent with historical case law, which in the vast majority of cases involved a Husband payor of alimony asserting a Wife’s cohabitation required relief from his support obligation. The Payor alleging cohabitation has the burden of proving *prima facie* showing of changed circumstances, and then the burden shifts to the receiver of alimony to prove that there was not a change of circumstance. See <u>Landau v. Landau</u>, 461 N.J. Super. 107, 118 (App.Div. 2019). Plaintiff asserts that the Court must evaluate Defendant’s demand for alimony in light of the New Jersey statute and the recent case law. Indeed, if the Court were required to issue an alimony order under circumstances, where cohabitation exists at time of trial, the result would be the payor of support would be left in the position of having to file an immediate post-judgment motion to terminate the award just entered. **<u>DISSIPATION</u>**

**<u>THE COURT MAY PROPERLY DENY DEFENDANT’S DEMAND FOR CREDITS BASED ON ALLEGED DISSIPATION BECAUSE PLAINTIFF PAID OFF HER STUDENT LOANS DURING THE MARRIAGE</u>**

Both parties entered the marriage with student loan debt. Plaintiff worked hard, furthered her education, thereby increasing her income. Her increased earnings were used to pay off her premarital debt over the course of the marriage. Evidence will show that this was the status quo. Defendant, who had separate finances, chose not to make payments towards his student loans, but only made the minimum payments. That is also the marital status quo. At no time did Defendant request funds to pay off his loans, yet he now demands post-Complaint for Plaintiff to pay-off his premarital debt. This must be rejected, as the Court cannot provide for Defendant a better arrangement than he provided for himself.

<u>N.J.S.A.</u> 2A:34-23.1(i) requires the court to consider the “contribution of each party to the acquisition, dissipation, preservation, depreciation or appreciation in the amount of value on the marital property.” Defendant alleges that Plaintiff dissipated marital property by paying off her student loans during the marriage. Plaintiff will prove that this is not the case. As an initial point, Defendant’s claim for dissipation must fail on procedural grounds as he never plead dissipation in his Answer and Counterclaim for Divorce. Defendant likewise never sought to Amend his pleadings to include dissipation. Thus, his claim of dissipation should be deemed waived.

In the unlikely event that the Court nevertheless entertains Defendant’s claim for dissipation, Plaintiff will provide proofs that both parties received a significant economic benefit from Plaintiff’s education, and that the repayment over time during the marriage was on ongoing marital expense and not a dissipation. Defendant cannot provide the Court with proofs that payment of student loan debt as a status quo expense constituted dissipation, such as in a case where there is a party who gambles.

The Courts in <u>Kothari v. Kothari</u>, 255 N.J. Super. 500 (App. Div. 1992) set forth the factors to be considered in determining a claim for dissipation. The Court directed that the following factors must be examined: (1) the proximity of the expenditure to the parties’ separation; (2) whether the expenditure was typical of expenditures made by the parties prior to the breakdown of the marriage, (3) whether the expenditure benefitted the “joint” marital enterprise or was for the benefit of one spouse to the exclusion of the other, and (4) the need for, and amount, of the expenditure. <u>Id.</u> at 507.

In this case, the student loans for Plaintiff’s MBA were used not just for her education but also for home renovations and did not have any proximity to the divorce. The expenditure was typical of costs incurred by Plaintiff as she had a college degree and another post-graduate degree. Plaintiff’s degree benefitted the marital enterprise because she was able to move into better, higher paying jobs. Lastly, the amount of the expenditure was a fixed amount, the student loan amount. The evidence will show that this claim is a red herring, meant solely for Defendant to capture a windfall for himself.

Courts have treated the issue of credits for student loans differently than other debts. In <u>Mahoney v. Mahoney</u>, 91 N.J. 488 (1982), the court determined that an MBA was not an asset that is subject to equitable distribution. This case, however, is clearly distinguishable from Plaintiff’s case on the facts. In <u>Mahoney</u>, a case which dates back forty years, one spouse made financial sacrifices so that the other could obtain an MBA. Based upon those facts, the Court provided reimbursement alimony for the party who did not earn the education but paid for it. Reimbursement alimony was created to help combat the concept that professional degrees and licenses are “property” subject to equitable distribution at the termination of a marriage. *See* <u>Mahoney</u> at 500-01. Reimbursement alimony is awarded appropriately to a spouse who has made financial sacrifices, resulting in a temporarily reduced standard of living, to allow the other spouse to secure an advanced degree or professional license to enhance the parties' future standard of living.  <u>Id</u>.

In this case, the evidence will prove that Defendant never paid for Plaintiff’s degree, and certainly never suffered a reduced standard of living even with her pay down of her loans. Defendant did not make any financial sacrifices nor did he modify his standard of living while Plaintiff pursued higher education. Plaintiff continued to work and contribute more than her fair share towards the household expenses during the time that she attended graduate school. She did not rely entirely on Defendant’s income during that period.

There are currently no published cases in New Jersey comparable to the facts in the current matter. An unpublished Appellate Division case arising out of Essex County, <u>McBoyle v. McBoyle</u>, No. A-2822-16T4T2, 2020 WL 2464761 (App. May 13, 2020), does present facts similar to the facts in the instant matter. See Case attached hereto as **<u>Exhibit B</u>**. Plaintiff advises that she is not aware of any subsequent case law that contradicts this unpublished opinion. While this case is not binding on this Court, the facts and analysis are instructive. Prior to the parties’ marriage, Mr. McBoyle was enrolled at [University] Business School. During the time that he was enrolled, his parents loaned him money in the amount of \$88,290 to pay for his tuition and expenses. This was to be repaid in as timely a period as possible at a 4.5% interest rate. Mr. McBoyle did not start to pay back the loan to his family until almost a decade later during the parties’ marriage. It totaled almost \$300,000.

The Appellate Court considered the exact issue presented by the instant case, namely, whether Mr. McBoyle’s payback of this pre-marital loan during the marriage could be considered dissipation at the time of the divorce. The Court concluded that the repayment of the loan during the marriage was not dissipation. In making this determination, the Court considered that the loan was paid back on a regular basis until the debt was paid back in full. Mr. McBoyle did not start to repay the loan only at the time of separation, but instead had been paying it consistently for four years before the breakdown. The facts are the same with Plaintiff and her responsible repayment of the student loan.

The <u>McBoyle</u> Court indicated that “although this is not a typical marital expense, student loan payments are necessarily unique because they correspond to an expense which is usually undertaken only once or twice in an individual’s lifetime.” <u>Id.</u> at 11. They distinguished <u>Monte</u> and instructed that “the loan facilitated the success of plaintiff’s prosperous career in finance, which benefitted the entire family. This is the type of loan that may be deemed a marital debt. It is self-evident that the opportunities afforded by a degree funded by this debt led to the lifestyle which the entire family enjoyed.” <u>Id</u>. at 11. The evidence will show facts similar to those in <u>McBoyle</u> occurred in this case. Plaintiff’s pursuit of her MBA, while it incurred some student loan debt paid during the marriage, also served as a trajectory for her career.

Plaintiff will provide evidence that both parties had student loans at the start of the marriage, and she paid hers off in 2011 from her income. Defendant chose to pay only the minimum required amount of his pre-marital student loans throughout the entirety of the marriage. From 2016 through 2018, Plaintiff attended [University] for her MBA. She took out a loan in the amount of \$122,000. She received \$60,000 from her employer for reimbursement of her education. She used approximately \$60,000 of her graduate student loans to renovate their home benefitting Defendant. In addition, throughout her graduate school education, she maintained full-time employment and continued to contribute towards the marital expenses as she had done prior to her enrollment. The evidence presented will show that the MBA loan was a legitimate marital debt properly repaid during the marriage. Similar to the facts in <u>McBoyle</u>, Plaintiff consistently paid of all her debt as it was incurred during the marriage. Upon her graduation in 2018, she paid off the loan in December 2018 (approximately two years prior to the Complaint for Divorce being filed). She borrowed the funds due from [Child]’s 529, and later she repaid the entire sum back to the 529 account from her earnings.

Plaintiff’s income significantly increased because of her continued education, benefitting the “joint” marital enterprise. Defendant’s demand for a dissipation credit must be denied.

**<u>MALLAMO CREDITS</u>**

**<u>THE COURT MUST AWARD MALLAMO CREDITS TO PLAINTIFF FOR EXCESS *PENDENTE LITE* SUPPORT, FOR DEFENDANT’S SHARE OF CHILD SUPPORT AND FOR DEFENDANT’S SHARE OF CHILD’S COLLEGE COSTS</u>**

Courts have the equitable power to establish alimony and support orders in connection with a pending matrimonial action. <u>Lepis v. Lepis</u>, 83 N.J. 139, 145 (1980). Orders for *pendente lite* support “are subject to modification prior to entry of final judgment.” <u>Mallamo v. Mallamo</u>, 280 N.J. Super. 8, 12 (App. Div. 1995). There, the Appellate Division retroactively reduced the amount of alimony during the *pendente lite* period and held that “\[t\]he State has long recognized the power of the judiciary to prevent irreparable harm and to preserve the *status quo* through the device of awarding temporary financial support pending a full investigation of a family case.” <u>Id.</u> at 11-12 (citing <u>Crowe v. DiGioia</u>, 90 N.J. 126, 132 (1982)). In its decision, the Court noted that when a trial court is granting *pendente lite* relief, there is often conflicting and incomplete information and the judge cannot create an accurate picture. Only after a trial can a “judge evaluate the evidence oral and documentary and weigh the credibility of the parties.” <u>Id.</u> at 16.

Here, the Defendant has received a total of \$128,000 in *pendente lite* support from August 1, 2020, through March 1, 2023. Additionally, he has made no financial contribution to [Child] since he moved from the marital residence in July 2020. These funds, combined with his own income, far exceed his needs. If the Court determines that Plaintiff has an ongoing monthly alimony obligation, Plaintiff should be credited for the support already provided to Defendant.

Based on the directives of <u>Mallamo</u> and its progeny, Plaintiff will prove from financials and testimony that Defendant received excess support over and above his need to maintain his marital lifestyle. In addition to this overpayment based on financial need, Plaintiff also seeks a reimbursement of the 31 months of support payments at \$4,000 per month because Defendant has been cohabitating. She seeks reimbursement retroactive to the date of her support payments in August 2020. If the Court issues such an order with a credit to Plaintiff, she would agree that child support due and owing her should be calculated without the \$4,000 as part of his available income.

**<u>COUNSEL AND EXPERT FEES</u>**

**<u>THE COURT MUST HOLD DEFENDANT RESPONSIBLE FOR PORTIONS OF PLAINTIFF’S ATTORNEY AND EXPERT FEES</u>**

Both parties have incurred significant counsel fees to litigate this divorce matter, primarily due to Defendant’s bad faith as discussed in detail above. Plaintiff has unnecessarily had to pay to defend multiple motions, pay multiple mediators, pay for experts, and cover legal fees for both parties for court appearances, ESPs and ISCs. Plaintiff will present evidence that Defendant litigated in bad faith, lied repeatedly, and took positions so unreasonable as to require a trial. While Plaintiff did not initially anticipate making a claim for counsel fees, the facts surrounding his cohabitation and excessive overspending on himself and his girlfriend compelled her to make this demand. Plaintiff initially had not requested an award of counsel fees, but did file a motion requesting to amend her pleading to include a request for counsel fees

Defendant should be responsible for a portion of Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees due to his bad faith. The Court has the authority to award counsel fees in a family action pursuant to <u>Rule</u> 4:42-9(a)(1), <u>Rule</u> 5:3-5(c), and <u>N.J.S.A</u>. 2A:34-23. One of the justifications behind an allowance of counsel fees in matrimonial litigation is to prevent a maliciously motivated party from inflicting economic damage on an opposing party by forcing expenditures for counsel fees. <u>Kelly v. Kelly</u>, 262 N.J. Super. 303, 307 (Ch. Div. 1992). <u>Rule</u> 5:3-5(c) makes clear that the analytic focus is not only to be based on the need and ability of a party to pay, but also on concepts of general fairness, which is linked to the broader systemic interest in limiting unnecessary or emotionally based litigation, conduct, and positions. Counsel fee awards may be made to deter inappropriate and unreasonable positions.

<u>Rule</u> 5:3-5(c) then confirms prior case law which focused, addressed, and recognized the importance of assessing good and bad faith. <u>See</u>, <u>e.g.</u>, <u>Salch v. Salch</u>, 240 N.J. Super. 441, 443 (App. Div. 1990) (the court held that in addition to the financial considerations the trial judge must take into account the good or bad faith of either party); <u>Kozak v. Kozak</u>, 280 N.J. Super. 272, 279 (1994) (the court properly awarded the wife counsel fees as a “necessary” expense to defend against her husband for legal representation where Mr. Kozak’s actions constituted a waste of the limited judicial resources in the family party).

Importantly, the focus of inquiry is now on the positions taken by a litigant, not the subjective motivation which led to the position being taken. <u>Rule</u> 5:3-5(c) provides for an award of attorney’s fees as follows:

> Subject to the provisions of <u>R</u>\[<u>ule</u>\] 4:42-9(b), (c), and (d), the \[C\]ourt in its discretion may make an allowance both *pendente lite* and on final determination, to be paid by any party in the action, including, if deemed to be just, any party successful in the action, on any claim for a divorce, dissolution of civil union, termination of domestic partnership, nullity, support, alimony, custody, parenting time, equitable distribution, separate maintenance, enforcement of agreements between spouses, domestic partners, or civil union partners and claims relating to family type matters. . . . . In determining the amount of the fee award, the \[C\]ourt should consider, in addition to the information required to be submitted pursuant to <u>R</u>\[<u>ule</u>\] 4:42-9, the following factors: (1) the financial circumstances of the parties; (2) the abilities of the parties to pay their own fees or to contribute to the fees of the other party; (3) the <u>reasonableness and good faith of the positions advanced by the parties both during and prior to trial</u>; (4) the extent of fees incurred by both parties; (5) any fees previously awarded; (6) amount of fees previously paid to counsel by each party; (7) the result obtained; (8) the degree to which fees were incurred to enforce existing order or to compel discovery, and (9) <u>any other factor bearing on the fairness of an award</u>.
>
> \[<u>Rule</u> 5:3-5(c) (emphasis added).\]

Plaintiff has meaningfully participated in all attempts to settle this matter, including mediation with two (2) different mediators and an ISC with the Court. She has also made several settlements offers. Defendant, on the other hand, has hidden relevant facts, such as his address out of state, his marriage-like relationship with his girlfriend and he has failed and refused to provide proofs of his actual expenses on his CIS. He has submitted three CISs and his claim of his marital lifestyle is disproved by the analysis of [Plaintiff’s Accounting Firm].

Defendant has misrepresented his expenses, has taken steps to conceal his cohabitation, and he has been untruthful throughout this case to pursue his demand for alimony to which he is not entitled by New Jersey law. He has outright refused to present reasonable positions throughout this litigation and he continues to live above and beyond the marital lifestyle. Due to Defendant’s bad faith, Plaintiff has incurred a substantial amount in attorney’s fees. Plaintiff will provide proofs and detail as to her total counsel fees incurred in her formal Request for Counsel Fees that will be submitted at the end of trial. Defendant should be responsible for his share of the expert fees and a portion of Plaintiff’s legal fees and costs.
