Defendant’s Motion for Reconsideration is not a valid motion for reconsideration, but rather, a thinly disguised, second bite at the apple to re-address relief that this Court already considered and resolved by way of its June 11, 2026 Order. In his Cross-Application dated April 14, 2026, and his Certification dated April 23, 2026, Defendant sought modification of child support, modification of custody and parenting time, cancellation and/or revision of the December 6, 2024 Order, and at least seventeen additional or restated forms of relief, all of which the Court considered and resolved in the June 11, 2026 Order. Defendant may not now reargue his Cross-Application and supporting submissions by way of a motion for reconsideration simply because he is dissatisfied with the result. For the reasons set forth below, and in the Certification of Plaintiff, Defendant’s motion should be denied in its entirety.

**<u>DEFENDANT HAS NOT IDENTIFIED ANY RULING DECIDED ON A PALPABLY INCORRECT BASIS OR ANY EVIDENCE THE COURT OVERLOOKED, AND HIS MOTION SHOULD BE DENIED.</u>**

Absent newly discovered evidence, or recent controlling case law that a litigant believes a court has overlooked, an application for reconsideration must be denied as a matter of law. <u>Cummings v. Bahr</u>, 295 N.J. Super. 374, 384 (App. Div. 1996).

It is well-settled that a motion for reconsideration is improper where the moving party is simply dissatisfied with the Court’s prior decision and seeks to reargue that very motion. <u>See</u> <u>D’Atria v. D’Atria</u>, 242 N.J. Super. 392, 401 (Ch. Div. 1990); <u>Michel v. Michel</u>, 210 N.J. Super. 218, 219 (Ch. Div. 1985).

Indeed, motions for reconsideration are disfavored by courts since they contravene well-established policy favoring finality of judicial determinations. <u>See</u> <u>Palumbo v. Twp. of Old Bridge</u>, 243 N.J. Super. 142, 147 n.3 (App. Div. 1990); <u>State v. Fitzsimmons</u>, 286 N.J. Super. 141, 147 (App. Div. 1995) (noting that the purpose of *R.* 4:49-2 “is not to re-argue the motion that has already been heard for the purpose of taking the proverbial second bite of the apple”).

Within the context of matrimonial litigation, it has been further averred that:

> Reconsideration should be utilized only for those cases which fall into that narrow corridor in which either (1) the Court \[\] expressed its decision based upon a palpably incorrect or irrational basis, or (2) it is obvious that the Court either did not consider, or failed to appreciate the significance of probative, competent evidence … if a litigant wishes to bring new or additional information to the court’s attention which it could not have provided on the first application, the court should, in the interest of justice (and in the exercise of sound discretion), consider the evidence. Nevertheless, motion practice must come to an end at some point, and if repetitive bites at the apple are allowed, the core will swiftly sour.

<u>D’Atria</u>, 242 N.J. Super. at 401–02.

In the present matter, Defendant continues to take “bites at the apple” despite the glaring absence of any of the above substantive requirements under <u>R.</u> 4:49-2 or the decisional law. The relief upon which Defendant’s motion is based, as set forth more fully in Plaintiff’s Certification, was already presented to, considered by, and adjudicated upon by the Court in the June 11, 2026 Order. Defendant presents no newly discovered evidence, no controlling law, and no fact overlooked or misapplied by the Court, other than his own disagreement with the Court’s determination. Defendant ignores the well-settled standard governing reconsideration and instead asks this Court, under the guise of reconsideration, to grant him a result he did not obtain the first time.

Defendant’s motion, although voluminous, likewise fails to comply with <u>R.</u> 4:49-2, which requires that a motion for reconsideration “state with specificity the basis on which it is made, including a statement of the matters or controlling decisions which counsel believes the court has overlooked or as to which it has erred.” Defendant does not identify a single matter or controlling decision the Court is alleged to have overlooked. To the contrary, Defendant’s own proposed form of Order leaves blank every line intended for the Court to identify which issues were purportedly “reconsidered” and how each should be resolved, confirming that Defendant has not undertaken, and cannot undertake, the showing <u>R.</u> 4:49-2 demands.

Because Defendant has not identified any newly discovered evidence, any controlling law overlooked by the Court, or any finding decided on a palpably incorrect or irrational basis, his motion falls squarely outside the narrow corridor in which reconsideration is available, and it should be denied in its entirety. <u>Cummings</u>, 295 N.J. Super. at 384; <u>D’Atria</u>, 242 N.J. Super. at 401.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, and for the reasons set forth in the Certification of Plaintiff submitted herewith, Plaintiff respectfully requests that the Court deny Defendant’s Motion for Reconsideration in its entirety, and award Plaintiff the reasonable counsel fees and costs incurred in opposing this motion.
