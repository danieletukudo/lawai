**<u>INTRODUCTION</u>**

The Plaintiff is represented by this firm, Callagy Law, P.C.; Tanya L. Freeman, Esq. has represented Plaintiff since the Complaint for Divorce was filed. The Defendant is represented by the DeTommaso Law Group LLC.[^1] Both attorneys have represented the parties since the inception of the matter.

As an initial matter, Plaintiff objects to Defendant’s summation on two (2) grounds. First, Defendant based his summation on audio recordings that counsel personally transcribed. It goes without saying that there is a significant reliability issue at play. Plaintiff requests that the Court strike all references to this self-created transcript in reviewing the summation because it is unreliable and does not comport with the instructions provided by the Court. On the final day of trial, the Court instructed the parties to include copies of all written transcripts if same was referenced.

As Plaintiff’s second procedural objection, Defendant includes citation to a transcript of a motion hearing that was neither used at trial by either party nor was moved into evidence. The mere fact that the transcript was marked as a trial exhibit, does not allow Defendant to ignore the Rules of Court and include such reference in his summation. Plaintiff likewise asks the Court to strike all references to the February 8, 2023 transcript. In addition, Defendant cites to exhibits that were contained in his trial summation but never marked in evidence.

Plaintiff in her review of Defendant’s summation, was taken aback by the personal attacks levied against her. Defendant’s strategy appears to be portraying Plaintiff as the scorned woman, when in fact she was a zealous advocate for her legal rights. Plaintiff’s righteous indignation was proper because Defendant (a) is not entitled to alimony based on not having a financial need and (b) he has no legal right to alimony based on his cohabitation under New Jersey law. Despite Defendant’s efforts to portray her as angry and bitter, the truth is this case would have never gone to trial but for his bad faith position both in law and in the concealment of relevant facts proving his cohabitation. Defendant’s exclusive relationship tantamount to marriage was evidenced through testimony, financial records, and travel documents. Plaintiff’s expert’s testimony likewise proved to the Court that Defendant could maintain the marital status quo on his income alone. [Plaintiff’s Expert]’s testimony was not refuted, Defendant and his expert failed to provide the court with a scintilla of proof concerning what portion of the marital disbursements were attributable to him, nor did they provide any evidence of what Defendant’s need for alimony would be. Shockingly, Defendant summarily requests that the court grant him \$12,000 per month despite no testimony, personal or expert, to support such a brazen demand.

Defendant’s cavalier and dismissive attitude was fully present during his testimony, both on direct and cross. He smugly admitted for the first time on the witness stand to the length and depth of his relationship. Had he been this forthright during the litigation, the trial would have never been necessary. Even when confronted with a motion to terminate pendente lite support, a few months before the first day of trial, Defendant remained steadfast in concealing his relationship. Defendant went to great lengths to have the Court believe that he was merely protecting his privacy and Plaintiff’s request for his full address was denied. His tactics were finally revealed on the witness stand and he admitted that the dire lengths he went to conceal his address was because he suspected private investigators were following him and he didn’t want to make it easy. This conduct should not go unnoticed by the Court as he appears with unclean hands. Defendant knew very well that cohabitation was at issue and he lied and fought vehemently to keep relevant facts hidden. Defendant’s obstructionist behavior extended to lying about his address not only in sworn court documents, but in his tax filings as well. The rouse, of living in New Jersey, at a P.O. Box is just the tip of the iceberg of damaging evidence presented by Plaintiff in her case against alimony.

Defendant characterizes Plaintiff as hostile and bitter, based on listening to the audio of her testimony. Plaintiff properly presented her facts with honesty and poise to support her legal positions in stark contrast to Defendant’s conduct. In light of the Defendant’s continued lies, omissions of relevant facts, and egotistical justification for his misconduct, Plaintiff righteous indignation was warranted.

The <u>S.W.</u> Court further instructs that a determination made by the Court as to marital lifestyle must be articulated numerically. The Appellate Division, in <u>S.W. v. G.M,</u> explicitly directs that “a finding of marital lifestyle must be made by explaining the characteristics of the lifestyle and quantifying it.” <u>S.W.</u>, 462 N.J. Super. At 532. This quantification requires the Court to consider the relevant testimony and to review CISs, as well as other financial documents and relevant evidence. <u>Id.</u> The factfinder may “accept or reject any portion of the marital lifestyle presented by a party or an expert or calculate the lifestyle using any combination of the presentations.” <u>Id.</u> In this case, Plaintiff’s testimony, financial records and proofs and the testimony of [Plaintiff’s Expert] of [Plaintiff’s Accounting Firm] to demonstrate (1) the parties’ respective incomes during the marriage and (2) that she spent a monthly average of \$35,987 compared to Defendant’s monthly average spending of \$7,668 each month.

The Court in <u>S.W.</u> makes clear that numerically articulating the marital standard does not mean, however, that the Court should simply apply the parties’ net incomes. There is <u>no indication that</u>

> the Legislature intended income equalization or a formulaic application in alimony cases, even where the parties spent the entirety of their income. Had the Legislature intended alimony be calculated through use of a formula, there would be no need for the statutory requirement that the trial court address all the statutory factors. The Legislature declined to adopt a formulaic approach to the calculation of alimony.
>
> \[<u>Id.</u> At 533 (citation omitted).\]

This instruction by the Court makes clear that income is not equalized and there is no easy formula or entitlement by a spouse. Defendant, in his demand for alimony, is attempting to circumvent the law on alimony as directed by the Appellate Division in 2020 in <u>S.W.</u> Neither Defendant nor his expert provide the Court with any information to determine Defendant’s alimony demand, and even if such a demand was stated there is no justification for an award of alimony in this case under <u>S.W.</u>

The <u>S.W.</u> Court makes clear that, during a marriage, some expenses incurred are not incurred by both parties, or by either party in some circumstances, such as child related costs. The Appellate Division stated that “\[t\]he portion of the marital budget attributable to a party is likewise not subject to a formula.” <u>Id.</u> The Court recognized that there are expenses that may not be associated with either party, “including those associated with children who have since emancipated or whose expenses are met by an asset or a third-party source having no bearing on alimony . . . . \[T\]here are \[also\] expenses which <u>only one party incurred</u> during the marriage.” <u>Id \[emphasis added\].</u>

Accordingly, the case law makes clear that the Court must analyze Defendant’s demand for alimony from Plaintiff by first determining the Defendant’s needs based on a review of his own expenses during the marriage. This analysis does not and cannot consider or include Plaintiff’s expenses or her status quo standard of living. In this marriage, Plaintiff spent almost five times the amount to maintain her lifestyle as did Defendant. Additionally, Defendant failed to present any evidence, by way of testimony or proofs, of what need actually exists. Defendant outright failed to distinguish between the joint marital lifestyle and the lifestyle that he individually maintained during the marriage.

The <u>S.W</u> Court instructs the trial court that it “must attribute the expenses that pertain to the supported spouse. Only then may the judge consider the supported spouse’s ability to contribute to his or her own expenses and the amount of alimony necessary to meet the uncovered sum.” <u>Id.</u> At 534. Once the Court has made a determination as to marital lifestyle, it “should review the adequacy and reasonableness of the support award against \[its\] finding.” <u>Crews</u>, 164 N.J. at 26.

Other issues considered by the Courts when reviewing a demand for alimony include the savings by the parties during the marriage. The Court considers whether the parties elected to amass savings during the marriage when determining the marital lifestyle. See <u>Lombardi v. Lombardi</u>, 447 N.J. Super. 26, 36-37 (App. Div. 2016). In this case, while both Defendant and Plaintiff had investment and retirement plans, neither party saved significantly considering their total income. Plaintiff testified that the amount of money that went into savings “varied month to month” after determining if there were any funds left after paying month household bills and credit card statements (**Transcript – July 25, 2023 Page 192, Lines 6-12**). Because savings was not a significant part of the Defendant and Plaintiff’s lifestyle, a savings component should not be factored into Defendant’s demand for alimony, should the Court consider same. This argument is further supported by Defendant’s most recent Case Information Statement, which was prepared in anticipation of Trial, which does not include a savings component other than Defendant’s retirement savings (**D-28**).

An additional consideration of the Court in analyzing an alimony determination is “the way the couple actually lived, whether they resorted to borrowing and parental support, or if they limited themselves to their income.” <u>Hughes v. Hughes</u>, 311 N.J. Super. 15, 34 (App. Div. 1998). In this case, the parties did not borrow and did not incur significant debt. They had car loans and a mortgage and Plaintiff paid her credit cards in full each month with her own income. (**Transcript – July 27, 2023 Page 45, Lines 18-20**).

Pursuant to Defendant’s testimony at Trial, Defendant’s present income is \$153,000 per year, and pursuant to Plaintiff’s expert report Defendant’s marital lifestyle totaled \$7,668 per month. Accordingly, Defendant is able to meet his own needs without support. Defendant does not need and is not entitled to alimony.

2.  **<u>The Court must deny Defendant’s demand for alimony because he is not entitled to support due to his cohabitation in a relationship tantamount to marriage.</u>**

New Jersey addresses the issue of cohabitation and the impact of cohabitation on a party’s entitlement to alimony pursuant to <u>N.J.S.A.</u> 2A:34-23(n). In this case, Plaintiff provided evidence that Defendant began a monogamous relationship over three and a half years ago, that he relocated from New Jersey to Texas to live in the same apartment complex as his girlfriend, Defendant concealed his relationship and location by refusing to provide his address to the Court as discussed herein, and that their relationship satisfies the statutory factors of cohabitation as discussed in detail below. The Appellate Division in 2021 issued a landmark opinion in a post-judgment matter on issue of cohabitation in its decision of <u>Temple v. Temple</u>, 468 <u>N.J. Super.</u> 364 (App. Div. 2021). The Court, after reviewing the statutory factors pertinent to a determination of cohabitation, defined cohabitation as “mutually supportive, intimate, personal relationship” in which the couple has “undertaken duties and privileges that are commonly associated with marriage or civil union.” This is consistent with historical case law, which in the vast majority of cases involved a Husband payor of alimony asserting a Wife’s cohabitation required relief from his support obligation. The Payor alleging cohabitation has the burden of proving *prima facie* showing of changed circumstances, and then the burden shifts to the receiver of alimony to prove that there was not a change of circumstance. See <u>Landau v. Landau</u>, 461 N.J. Super. 107, 118 (App.Div. 2019). Plaintiff asserts that the Court must evaluate Defendant’s demand for alimony in light of the New Jersey statute and the recent case law. Plaintiff strongly asserts that Defendant’s cohabitation during the *pendente lite* period allows the Court to make a ruling that he is not entitled to support. There is no New Jersey precedent requiring an alimony award in the facts presented in this case, and <u>N.J.S.A.</u> 2A:34-23(n) does not state that the Court must turn a blind eye to ongoing cohabitation if it is presented at the time of trial as opposed to post-judgment. While <u>N.J.S.A.</u> 2A:34-23(n) applies to the award of alimony, and there is no case law applying the statute to *pendente lite* support, the facts in this case are so egregious; Defendant admitted to starting his relationship simultaneous with his relocation to [City B], Texas and continuing his relationship through present. **<u>MALLAMO CREDITS</u>**

1.  **<u>The Court must award *Mallamo* credits to Plaintiff for excess *pendente lite* support paid to Defendant.</u>**

As an initial matter, the parties resolved the issues of credits owed by Defendant to Plaintiff for Defendant’s share of child support and for Defendant’s share of Child’s college costs; based on the income determined as part of the Final Judgment of Divorce, Plaintiff and Defendant will share costs proportionally.

At trial, proofs were presented that Defendant had the ability to pay his expenses commensurate with those of the joint marital lifestyle with his income of \$153,000, namely the Marital Disbursement Analysis prepared by Plaintiff’s expert which outlined Defendant’s expenditures during the marriage (**P-179**). He used the additional funds not to maintain the marital lifestyle, but to enjoy a lifestyle far above his marital lifestyle. Plaintiff spent over 84% of the income, most of which she earned herself and kept in her own accounts. Defendant spent his own income which he also kept in his own accounts. New Jersey law supports Plaintiff’s claim for reimbursement.

Courts have the equitable power to establish alimony and support orders in connection with a pending matrimonial action. <u>Lepis v. Lepis</u>, 83 N.J. 139, 145 (1980). Orders for *pendente lite* support “are subject to modification prior to entry of final judgment.” <u>Mallamo v. Mallamo</u>, 280 N.J. Super. 8, 12 (App. Div. 1995). There, the Appellate Division retroactively reduced the amount of alimony during the *pendente lite* period and held that “\[t\]he State has long recognized the power of the judiciary to prevent irreparable harm and to preserve the *status quo* through the device of awarding temporary financial support pending a full investigation of a family case.” <u>Id.</u> at 11-12 (citing <u>Crowe v. DiGioia</u>, 90 N.J. 126, 132 (1982)). In its decision, the Court noted that when a trial court is granting *pendente lite* relief, there is often conflicting and incomplete information and the judge cannot create an accurate picture. Only after a trial can a “judge evaluate the evidence oral and documentary and weigh the credibility of the parties.” <u>Id.</u> at 16.

Here, the Defendant has received a total of \$156,000 in *pendente lite* support from August 1, 2020, through October 1, 2023. Additionally, he has made no financial contribution to Child since he moved from the marital residence in July 2020. These funds, combined with his own income, far exceed his needs. If the Court determines that Plaintiff has an ongoing monthly alimony obligation, Plaintiff should be credited for the support already provided to Defendant.

Based on the directives of <u>Mallamo</u> and its progeny, Plaintiff proved that Defendant received excess support over and above his need to maintain his marital lifestyle. In addition to this overpayment based on financial need, Plaintiff also seeks a reimbursement of the 39 months of support payments at \$4,000 per month because Defendant has been cohabitating. She seeks reimbursement retroactive to the date of her support payments in August 2020. If the Court issues such an order with a credit to Plaintiff, she would agree that child support due and owing her should be calculated without the \$4,000 as part of his available income.
